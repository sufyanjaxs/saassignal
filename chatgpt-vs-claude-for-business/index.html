ChatGPT vs Claude for Business Writing: Which One We'd Buy

Two assistants, the same 14 writing jobs, one week. One of them quietly became the default.

Sufyan Khan · Updated 2026-09-28 · 8 min read

The comparison posts on this site usually end with a table and no decision. This one ends

with a decision, because that is the useful part.

Method

Fourteen real writing jobs over one week: three 2,000-word research briefs, five customer

reply sets that needed care rather than speed, two incident write-ups, a quarterly summary

drafted from a spreadsheet, three short-form social sets, and one genuinely awkward email

to a client about a delay. Same source material, same constraints, both tools, one

pass each. No cross-polishing of the losing output.

The results that mattered

Instruction following

Both followed explicit constraints most of the time. The difference showed up in

constraints that were stated but implied. When told to keep a brief under 2,000 words and

preserve all six required data points, one tool cut the word count consistently; the other

hit the word count and dropped a required figure. For regulated or client work, that

difference is the whole game.

Voice consistency across a long piece

Across a 2,000-word brief with a defined house voice, one tool held the register from

paragraph two to the end. The other drifted toward generic marketing cadence around the

middle — the drift was subtle enough that it would have passed review without a human

edit. Over a 400-word email, neither drifted. Drift scales with length.

Editing distance

We counted how much of the generated text survived to final. Research briefs: one tool

kept roughly 70% with light editing, the other around 55%. Customer replies where tone

mattered: both high, because the source material constrained the voice. Spreadsheet to

summary: near-identical. The gap is real but narrow, and it exists specifically on long

generative work.

Cost predictability

Both bill by tokens, but the practical difference is cap behaviour. One gives a lower

ceiling that you hit and can plan around. The other gives a higher ceiling that arrives

without warning mid-task. For a team with a fixed software budget, predictability beat

raw capability.

What ChatGPT still wins

TaskWhy ChatGPT wins
Anything with an imageGeneration, editing and analysis in one thread
Spreadsheet reasoningReads, writes and explains sheets without leaving the chat
Multi-modal inputPDFs, screenshots and images in the same workflow
Ecosystem breadthImage gen, voice, plugins, one subscription

If your work involves an image or a spreadsheet in the same week, this is not close. The

capability difference is larger than the writing quality difference.

What Claude wins

Document-length work, code review, structured editing, and anything where a specific voice

must survive 2,000 words. Also the more predictable bill, which for a finance-approved

budget is not a small thing.

Our recommendation

**Buy one team seat on Claude for anyone whose job is producing long documents. Buy ChatGPT

for anyone whose work involves images or data.** Most small teams can justify both at

~25/seat/month — that is less than one hour of a contractor's time, and the writing hours

it returns are measured in tens per month.

What we would not do: buy both company-wide on day one, or pay for two overlapping tools

and use neither deeply. Start with the team plan, one seat per person, and expand when

someone can name what the tool did for them last week.

Before you buy

Two things that decide whether the seat pays for itself: a defined voice document or style

guide, and a list of three recurring tasks with a countable outcome. With both, we can

measure the ROI in two weeks. Without them, you are paying a subscription for the feeling

of productivity, and neither tool is good enough to hide that.
