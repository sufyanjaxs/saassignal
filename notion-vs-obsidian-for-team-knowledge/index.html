Notion vs Obsidian for a Team Knowledge Base: Six Months In

We ran both in parallel for six months. One became the memory of the company and the other became a graveyard of orphaned notes.

Knowledge base decisions are usually made by whoever is most excited about the tool. We

ran both for six months with the same team and the same content, then looked at what

people actually opened.

The setup difference is not the point

Notion got live in an afternoon. Templates, permissions and sharing worked immediately, and

within a week the team was writing in it without being asked.

Obsidian took about four hours of actual configuration to be useful — vault structure,

templates, plugin choices, and a decision about how notes link. But it became genuinely

better at week three, and stayed better for individuals writing dense, private notes.

That is the honest summary: Notion wins the first week, Obsidian wins the sixth month for

the person doing the thinking.

Search is where most knowledge bases actually fail

This is the test that mattered most, because a knowledge base nobody can search is a

write-only archive.

Both were better than any built-in search in the previous tool we used, and both failed

the same test: natural-language recall. "Find me the note about the pricing change we

discussed with the client in March" defeats keyword search on both platforms. The fix was

identical on both — a strict note template with a date, a client name field, and a

one-line summary at the top. Discipline, not software.

Permissions and the things you cannot undo

Notion's permission model is the strongest of the two. Per-page sharing, per-space roles,

and a clear view of who has access to what. For a knowledge base containing customer

details and pricing, that visibility was decisive.

Obsidian's model is filesystem permissions, which are powerful and completely unmanaged. We

had one incident where a sensitive folder was synced to a machine that should not have had

it. Nothing was breached, but the review process was uncomfortable — because with

filesystem access there is no answer to "who has access to this page" without checking

everyone's machine.

If your knowledge base contains anything you would not paste into a public Slack channel,
choose the tool that can answer "who has access" in one click.

Data ownership and the lock-in question

Notion is a closed platform. Export gives you Markdown and CSV — usable, lossy for

databases and formulas, and the result is not a working equivalent of what you had.

Obsidian is local Markdown files. You own them completely; delete the app and everything

still opens. That has real value, and it is the reason some teams will never move off it

regardless of features.

We tested the Notion export honestly: it worked, and re-importing it into Obsidian gave us

usable notes and broken database views. If your Notion workspace is database-heavy, plan

for real migration work — a day or two per hundred database entries, not an afternoon.

Performance and scale

At around 2,000 notes both were fine in daily use. At 5,000+ notes Notion's editor showed

noticeable lag on database-heavy pages, and Obsidian needed aggressive settings tuning plus

a community plugin for search speed. Neither is the bottleneck you should worry about

before 10,000 notes.

The honest recommendation

Use Notion if the knowledge base is genuinely shared — onboarding, runbooks, project

context. Its permissions and collision handling are the reason. The friction of asking

people to install a local app is the single biggest reason knowledge bases fail at

small companies, and Notion removes it.

Use Obsidian if you need one system for deep personal notes alongside team docs, if

your content touches code, or if data ownership is a hard requirement for your clients.

The best answer for many teams is to stop trying to make one tool do both jobs. Shared

team knowledge in Notion, individual thinking in Obsidian, and a rule about what crosses

between them. That sounds like two systems to maintain and it is — but it matched how

people actually worked, which no single tool did.
