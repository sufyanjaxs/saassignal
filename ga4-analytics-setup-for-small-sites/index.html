A GA4 Setup That Survives Contact With Real Traffic

Most small sites are running GA4 defaults and calling it analytics. Here is the setup that answers real questions.

Default GA4 works out of the box, which is why most small sites never look past the

default traffic report. The default report answers "how many people visited", which nobody

acts on. This is the setup that answers questions you can act on.

Step 1: Decide what a conversion is before you install anything

This is the step everyone skips, and it is why their analytics are useless. Write down the

two or three actions that mean the site is working for you. For a monetized content site

that is typically:

  1. Newsletter signup
  2. Outbound click to a monetized destination — affiliate link, product page
  3. Return visit of 3+ sessions in 30 days (a proxy for habit)

Pick three. Not twelve. Analytics configured against twelve goals tells you nothing, because

no single one moves enough to read a trend.

Step 2: Track them as events, not pageviews

GA4 defaults track outbound links as pageviews, which means your most valuable actions are

buried in a report you will never open. Create three custom events:

EventFires whenWhy it matters
newsletter_signupForm submitted successfullyPrimary conversion
outbound_clickAny click to a monetized domain with the tag parameterRevenue proxy
scroll_50User reaches 50% of an articleDepth signal

For outbound clicks, tag the links so you can attribute by article and by destination:

<a href="https://vendor.com/deal" rel="sponsored nofollow" data-ga="outbound_click"
   data-ga-dest="vendor">Get started</a>

Then in GA4: Admin → Events → mark outbound_click as a key event, and set the

destination as a custom parameter. You now know which article produced which click, not

just that a click happened.

Step 3: Fix the traffic attribution, or stop trusting it

GA4's default attribution is last-click with a 30-day window and modelled conversions that

can invent a conversion nobody made. Two settings to change:

  • Attribution model: if you ever run ads, set it to data-driven. If you do not, switch

to first-party and stop pretending the numbers are precise.

  • Modeled conversions: keep them, but never report them as real. Check the

"observed" versus "modeled" split before drawing a conclusion from a conversion rate.

The honest framing: GA4 tells you direction reliably and exact numbers unreliably. Use it for

"did that change help or hurt", never for "we made $412.80 yesterday".

Step 4: Build four reports and stop looking at the rest

Building more reports produces more places to not look at anything. Four is enough.

  1. Landing pages by engaged session rate. Sessions that lasted 10+ seconds or had 2+ pageviews, by landing page. This is the only traffic report worth daily attention.
  2. Newsletter funnel. Sessions on any article → reached the signup form (scroll event or view of the form) → completed signup. The gap between step two and three is the highest-value fix available to you.
  3. Top content by key events. Not by pageviews — by key events per session. A page with 400 views and 12 signups beats a page with 3,000 views and zero.
  4. Traffic by country. Because RPM varies 3–10× by geography and this determines whether your traffic is worth anything.

What to check weekly, and what to ignore

Check weekly: landing-page engagement, signup completions, top content by key event,

country mix. That is fifteen minutes and it covers ninety percent of decisions.

Ignore entirely: real-time screens (misleading at low volume), the default "engagement"

report, audience demographics beyond country, and any number under 500 sessions. Numbers

that small move randomly, and treating them as signal is how people conclude a redesign

failed when nothing happened.

Common mistakes that produce wrong numbers

  • Counting a signup when the form opens. Count the success event only — this inflates your conversion rate and can be an AdSense policy problem too.
  • One page = one session assumption. Single-page apps and infinite scroll break GA4's pageview model entirely; if you have either, this setup needs a plugin fix before any of the above is accurate.
  • Filtering out your own traffic on day one. Do it, but not permanently. Your own sessions are part of understanding whether the site is usable.
  • Trusting organic search clicks. Search Console is the source of truth for clicks and impressions. GA4 cannot see them reliably and should not be asked to.

The reporting cadence that works

Weekly, fifteen minutes, four reports. Monthly, thirty minutes, one question: which three

articles brought in key events per session, and what do those three have in common? Then

write more of those. That is the entire loop, and it works for a site with 200 visitors a

day as well as one with 200,000.
