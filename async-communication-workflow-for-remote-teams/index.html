The Async Communication Workflow That Fixed Our Meeting Load

We removed two recurring meetings a week and shipped more. Here is the system, with the failure modes.

"Go async" is advice given by people who have not had to coordinate three time zones. We

tried it properly, kept what worked, and here is the specific system — including the parts

that broke.

The problem we were solving

Thirty-seven recurring meetings a week for a nine-person team. Of those, our own audit

found that eleven had no agenda, six had a single decision owner not present, and four

were status meetings that could have been a written update. The cost was not just the hours

— it was that most decisions were made verbally and then reconstructed from memory,

producing at least two significant disagreements about what had been agreed.

The system

1. Every decision has one canonical written home

A single decisions/ folder in our knowledge base. One file per decision, named

YYYY-MM-DD-short-title, with four fields and nothing else:

  • Context — two paragraphs maximum on what forced the decision
  • Options — what we considered, including what we rejected
  • Decision — one sentence, in the present tense, stating what is now true
  • Revisit date — if this is a reversible decision, when we will check it

The single most important rule: once a decision is written there, it is true, and arguing

with it means superseding it — which means a new file, not an edit. Editing history is what

let the old verbal decisions drift into three versions.

2. Async is the default, meetings are the exception

A meeting needs three things to be worth the calendar slot: a decision that cannot be made

in writing, a disagreement that cannot be resolved in writing, or a conversation that

builds a relationship you will need later. Everything else is a document.

This is the rule that took longest to hold, and the reason it eventually did: we asked

anyone scheduling to state in the invite which of the three it was. "Sync" as a subject

line got declined and sent back.

3. Deadlines are local, availability overlaps deliberately

We have no company-wide working hours. What we have is four hours of deliberate overlap

per week, plus a rule that every task has a local-time deadline rather than an implied

"end of day". Tasks live in the shared tracker with an owner and a date, not in a

conversation.

4. Decisions get a written restart

Every Monday, one person posts a 200-word summary: what was decided, what is blocked, what

needs a decision inside seven days. It takes ten minutes and it is the single artefact that

makes async work. New people and returning people get current in one read.

We shipped more in the eight weeks after the change than in the eight weeks before, which

we did not expect.

The three failure modes, all of which we hit

Silence mistaken for agreement

The default failure. Someone does not reply, the deadline passes, and the work either

happens without the input or does not happen at all. Our fix was explicit: silence on a

decision is not consent, and the owner must chase named people after two working days. The

chasing is the system — it is uncomfortable and it works.

Async used to avoid hard conversations

This is the failure that can kill an async culture. People schedule a call to avoid

writing "I disagree with this approach". We watched that happen and fixed it by making

written disagreement explicitly normal and expected — the decisions/ files now contain

rejected options with reasons, which made writing a disagreement feel like normal

documentation rather than an act of opposition.

Documentation debt

Async work creates documents, and documents nobody maintains become worse than no

documents. We schedule one 45-minute session a week to prune decisions folder. Without it

the folder becomes a graveyard and trust in it decays. This is the maintenance cost of the

system and it is not optional.

What we did not do

  • No mandatory response window. We tried "everyone must reply within 24 hours." It

manufactured activity and produced low-quality replies to meet a quota. Now replies are

required only where a decision owner has asked for one.

  • No async standups in a chat channel. They just became a second meeting. The Monday

written restart replaced them entirely.

  • No recording. Recording meetings you were supposed to have avoided preserves the

meeting problem with extra steps.

If you adopt one thing

The written restart. Everything else in this system can be adopted piecemeal, and teams

that try to change all of it at once fail. A weekly 200-word summary of decisions and

blockers costs one person ten minutes a week and makes an entire team asynchronous within

a month.
